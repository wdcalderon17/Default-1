Texas Statute of Limitations: How Long Do You Have to File a Personal Injury Claim?

In Texas, most personal injury victims have two years to file a lawsuit. Missing this deadline can forfeit your right to compensation forever.

One of the most important — and most frequently misunderstood — aspects of Texas personal injury law is the statute of limitations. This law sets a strict deadline on how long you have to file a lawsuit after being injured. Miss this deadline and you may permanently lose your right to seek compensation, no matter how clear-cut the other driver's fault may be.

The Texas Two-Year Rule

Under Texas Civil Practice and Remedies Code § 16.003, most personal injury claims must be filed within two years of the date the cause of action accrued — which is typically the date of the accident or injury.

This two-year rule applies to:

  • Car and truck accident claims
  • Motorcycle accident claims
  • Slip and fall / premises liability claims
  • Workplace injury third-party claims
  • Most other personal injury cases

Wrongful Death Claims

Wrongful death claims in Texas also carry a two-year statute of limitations, but the clock runs from the date of death — not necessarily the date of the accident that caused the death.

Important Exceptions That Can Shorten Your Deadline

While two years sounds like plenty of time, certain situations can dramatically shorten your deadline:

Claims Against Government Entities

If your injury was caused by a Texas government entity — a city, county, TxDOT, a public school district, or a state agency — the Texas Tort Claims Act imposes much stricter requirements. You may need to file a formal notice of claim within 6 months of the incident. Failure to provide timely notice can bar your entire claim.

Medical Malpractice

Medical malpractice claims have a two-year statute of limitations from the date of the negligent act, but require a 60-day expert report notice before filing suit.

Minors

If the injured person is a minor (under 18), the statute of limitations is generally tolled (paused) until they reach adulthood. However, exceptions exist, particularly for claims against government entities.

Why You Should Act Now — Not Later

Even if you have two years, waiting is almost always harmful to your case. Here's why:

  • Evidence disappears. Surveillance videos are overwritten, witnesses forget details, and physical evidence degrades.
  • Insurance investigations start immediately. The at-fault party's insurer begins building their defense right away. You need an attorney doing the same for you.
  • Medical documentation becomes harder to obtain. Proving your injuries are tied to the accident gets harder with time.
  • Witnesses become harder to locate. People move, lose contact information, and memories fade.

Don't Rely on the Statute of Limitations as a Strategy

Many injury victims think: "I have two years, I'll deal with it later." This is one of the most common mistakes we see. By the time they contact an attorney a year or 18 months after the accident, key evidence is often gone and their case is far weaker than it would have been if they had acted promptly.
