Houston Public Transportation Injury Lawyers

Accidents involving Houston Metro buses, METRORail, and other public transportation present unique legal challenges — including strict notice requirements and immunity rules. Our attorneys know how to navigate these cases.

Types of Public Transportation Accidents We Handle

Public transportation serves millions of Houston-area residents every year. When accidents occur on these systems, the injuries can be serious — and the legal process is significantly different from a standard car accident claim. Calderon Legal Group handles public transportation injury cases involving:

  • Houston Metro (METRO) bus accidents — collisions, passenger falls, sudden stops and starts, door incidents, and accidents at bus stops
  • METRORail and light rail accidents — collisions at grade crossings, platform accidents, and train-versus-vehicle collisions along the Red, Green, Purple, and Silver lines
  • METROLift and paratransit accidents — specialized transportation for disabled and elderly passengers; often involves different operational standards and liability rules
  • Texas A&M and Bryan-College Station campus transit — university bus systems operating in the Bryan-College Station area; may involve mixed governmental and private liability
  • Rideshare vehicles used as transit (Uber, Lyft) — when TNC vehicles are involved in accidents during public transit-style operations
  • School bus accidents — involving public school district buses; subject to governmental immunity rules similar to METRO claims

Special Rules for Suing Government Transit Agencies

Houston Metro (Metropolitan Transit Authority of Harris County) is a governmental entity. Suing a government agency in Texas is fundamentally different from suing a private individual or company because of the doctrine of governmental immunity — the legal principle that the government cannot be sued without its consent.

Texas has partially waived governmental immunity through the Texas Tort Claims Act (Tex. Civ. Prac. & Rem. Code, Chapter 101). This law allows injury claims against government entities in certain circumstances — including motor vehicle accidents and accidents involving government-owned property — but imposes strict procedural requirements and damage caps that do not apply to private defendants.

Key rules under the Texas Tort Claims Act include:

  • Formal written notice requirement — before filing suit against a Texas governmental entity, you must provide formal written notice of your claim within 6 months of the incident (some entities may have shorter notice periods in their governing charters)
  • Damage caps — recovery against a governmental unit is capped at $250,000 per person and $500,000 per single occurrence for personal injury or death; $100,000 for property damage
  • No punitive damages — punitive or exemplary damages are not available against governmental entities under the Texas Tort Claims Act

Common Causes of Public Transit Accidents

  • Driver negligence — bus or rail operators speeding, running red lights, failing to yield, or driving while distracted
  • Inadequate vehicle maintenance — mechanical failures including brake failures, door malfunctions, and defective restraint systems
  • Dangerous bus stops and station conditions — wet or slippery surfaces, poor lighting, inadequate barriers, and unsafe platform conditions
  • Sudden stops and starts — passengers standing or moving when the vehicle brakes abruptly or accelerates suddenly
  • Failure to accommodate passengers with disabilities — improper securing of wheelchairs, failure to deploy ramps, and failure to assist disabled riders
  • Grade crossing accidents — METRORail collisions with vehicles at intersections where signals or barriers are inadequate

Who Is Responsible?

Determining who is legally responsible for a public transportation injury requires analyzing all parties involved:

  • Houston Metro (METRO) — as the transit agency, METRO may be liable for its drivers' negligence and its failure to maintain safe vehicles and facilities under the Texas Tort Claims Act
  • Individual transit drivers — in some circumstances, drivers can be held personally liable, particularly if the governmental entity denies liability or the driver's actions were outside the scope of their employment
  • Vehicle and equipment manufacturers — if a mechanical defect contributed to the accident, a product liability claim may be available against the manufacturer
  • Third-party drivers — if another vehicle caused the accident involving the transit vehicle, that driver's private insurance is fully available without governmental immunity limitations
  • Property owners — if a dangerous condition at a bus stop or transit station (owned by a private party) contributed to your injury, a premises liability claim may apply

Why You Must Act Quickly

Public transportation injury cases have stricter and shorter deadlines than standard personal injury claims. The most critical is the 6-month formal written notice requirement under the Texas Tort Claims Act. This notice must be provided to the governmental entity before you can file a lawsuit. Missing this deadline — even by a single day — can permanently bar your claim, regardless of how serious your injuries are.

Beyond the notice deadline, acting quickly is essential to:

  • Preserve surveillance footage from the bus, rail vehicle, station, or nearby businesses before it is overwritten
  • Obtain the transit agency's incident reports and maintenance records before they are unavailable
  • Identify and interview witnesses while memories are fresh
  • Document the scene and any dangerous conditions before they are corrected
